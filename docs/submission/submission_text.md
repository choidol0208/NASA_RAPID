## High-Level Summary

Fire Evidence Explorer is a web dashboard over NASA's microgravity combustion experiments. You describe what you are building and where, for example a lunar habitat, and it shows which recorded NASA tests bear on six fire-safety questions, and where that evidence stops.

The challenge calls this data hard to find, compare and interpret. FIND: a rule-based ranking orders the 24 combustion investigations of NASA's Physical Sciences Informatics (PSI) archive against your environment. COMPARE: 1,824 tests from NASA's tables share one table and counting rule. INTERPRET: per question it quotes published results, then says whether the data are in NASA's tables, only in the raw archive (found through NASA's test identifiers), or have no record in PSI (listing of 14 November 2026). For a lunar habitat, at 30% oxygen or more and at or below 70.3 kPa, there is no counted test (one whose stated purpose is the question).

A local open-weight model writes cited card summaries and, in a local install, runs a prototype assistant; code checks every sentence against its sources.

Designers and researchers learn what to read first. It is a research exploration tool, not safety guidance; counts and rankings are the team's computation, not NASA's conclusions.

## Project Details

### What it does

Fire Evidence Explorer is a dashboard page and a method page (`how.html`) of fixed files, with no server program behind them. It loads its own data files and calls no language model. A conversational assistant runs on the user's own computer with a local model (see "The assistant" below).

Words used below, each explained once:

- **Microgravity**: near-weightlessness, as on the International Space Station (ISS). A flame there has no buoyancy: hot gas does not rise away from it.
- **PSI**: Physical Sciences Informatics, the archive where NASA publishes the data of these experiments. An **investigation** is one experiment programme in PSI, for example BASS-II or FLEX. A **test** is one run of an experiment: one row of a NASA table. PSI also holds **raw files** (videos, archives, sensor workbooks, test sheets) that no table summarises.
- **kPa**: kilopascal, a unit of pressure. Air at sea level has 21% oxygen at 101.3 kPa (14.7 psi).
- **Need** (or fire-safety question): one question a designer asks, for example "down to what oxygen level does this material keep burning?". The page works with six needs.
- **Counted test**: a test that the page counts for a need. The rule is under Step 2.
- **Role**: one of four AI coding sessions (Claude Code) that built the repository, each owning part of it: pm, data, ui and catalog. A role is not a person; the team's human leads directed the sessions.
- **Range**: the oxygen and pressure limits that the team set for an environment, for example the lunar habitat. A test is **inside** when both its recorded oxygen and its recorded pressure lie in the range.

You type what you are building and where. Rough input is fine, for example "moon cotton fabric" or "30% O2 at 10.2 psi". Six buttons give ready examples: "ISS cabin", "Lunar habitat", "Lunar habitat, cotton fabric", "Smoke detection on the ISS", "Spacesuit" and "Mars base".

A sticky bar at the top of the page holds the search, "Copy link" (the query travels as `?q=`), "How it works" and "Source code". After a query, a row of summary tiles ("At a glance": the three closest investigations with their match scores, how many of the six needs have counted tests in the environment's range, and where the raw data are) sits above five tabs: "Closest investigations", "What was found", "Where the data are", "Where the evidence stops" and "All investigations". The two method sections, "How AI is used here, and how it was checked" and "How the score works", are on the method page `how.html`, behind the "How it works" button. A footer carries the notices and the build stamp.

The page first shows how it read your input (tab "Closest investigations"). Each condition is marked as typed by you, taken from a preset, or a default. Words that match nothing in PSI are named, so you know they changed nothing. For an environment with lunar or Martian gravity, the page warns that PSI holds no partial-gravity combustion data. When the input names no material, the page warns that the fuel is not compared and asks for one: such a ranking rests on the atmosphere, the flow and the purpose only.

Then the page answers in three steps.

**Step 1. FIND: the information you need.**

- Three result cards, for the closest of the 24 PSI combustion investigations.
- On each card, open: a match score from 0 to 100, the closest test configuration, the similarity on each condition, why it matched, the largest gap and the cautions. A score orders the investigations for one question only. Scores of different questions are never compared. The card's longer parts (the team note, the source links, the raw-file line, the "Weight check" line and "What the record says") open behind one disclosure per card, "Details and sources".
- A table "Requirements read from your question": each condition the query set (for example oxygen 30%), and for each result whether it covers it. The status says what the check rests on: "met" (a per-test table value or the catalog's classification), "documented" (a NASA document states it, but no per-test value shows it), "near" (a similarity of 0.9 or more), "not met" or "unverified" (no value, an assumed value or a team reading).
- Links to the PSI page, the dataset DOI (the permanent identifier that PSI asks users to cite), the source table and the catalog row.
- One line that says which raw data files the PSI archive lists for the investigation. The team counted them from PSI's file listing.
- For four example queries ("ISS", "lunar habitat", "spacesuit", "Mars base"), a "Weight check" line: how often the result keeps its place when the team's weights are drawn at random. Other queries, "lunar habitat cotton fabric" among them, have no such line.
- "What the record says": the objective as summarised in the team's catalog, the findings stated in the record or table, a short written summary, and NASA's own sentences, each quoted with the name of the record field it comes from.
- The written summary is labelled as AI-generated, with the model's name and with who checked it. Every sentence of it cites the NASA sentences it rests on. 20 of the 24 cards carry such a summary. The other 4 show NASA's sentences only.
- A full ranking of all 24 investigations, in the tab "All investigations".
- For the ISS cabin and the lunar habitat, downloads: the matched tests (CSV), an evidence package (JSON), all recorded tests (CSV), every value with its source column (CSV) and the list of dataset DOIs to cite.

**Step 2. COMPARE: the points to consider.**

The page works with six fire-safety information needs:

- Prevention: flammability limit; ignition; flame spread and growth.
- Suppression: extinction conditions (when does a flame go out).
- Detection: combustion products; smoke generation conditions.

The six needs were written with an AI assistant (Claude Code) from NASA standards and studies, under the team's direction. The list is the team's, not NASA's.

One rule decides what is counted. A test counts for a need only when the investigators or a NASA project document state that need as a purpose of the experiment. A test that records the right values without such a statement is marked "Outside stated objective" and is not counted. Below, "counted test" always has this meaning.

The tab "What was found" shows, after the published results (below, "Results first"):

- A table with one row per ranked investigation and one column per need. Each cell says whether the PSI table records the conditions and the outcome that the need asks for.
- For the ISS cabin and the lunar habitat, for each need: how many counted tests fall in the range of the environment, and what such a count means.
- For the same two environments, a side-by-side block. Each count links to its row in the team's data file.
- For the same two environments, the section "Recorded tests in the scenario range". Each count there opens a list of test ids. Each test id links to its row in the repository copy of the NASA PSI table.

Counts per environment exist for these two environments only. For any other input, the page still ranks the investigations and shows the needs table, the criteria list and the grids.

**Step 3. INTERPRET: the constraints, and where the data are.**

A constraint here is something a designer has to take into account. The page sets no design limit of its own and judges no test against a criterion.

For the ISS cabin and the lunar habitat, the page goes need by need. Each need opens into four parts, kept apart:

1. **What NASA's recorded tests showed.** Code computes these values from the PSI tables, for example how many tests ended in each recorded way. Each value comes with its number of tests and a note that says what the value is and what it is not. These are observed values. They are not limits, thresholds or requirements.
2. **Criteria in NASA documents.** What NASA standards and studies state for the need, in two lists: criteria, requirements and definitions; and questions and research needs named in the documents. Each item carries a tag (criterion or stated gap), its document and the place in it, with a link where the entry has one. The wording of each item was transcribed with an AI assistant.
3. **Published by NASA and the investigators (not in PSI's experimental tables).** Results and caveats from papers and reports, quoted with the document and with how much of it was read. They are quotes. They are never counted as tests. On 15 November the data role added 66 published results from 21 documents on the NASA Technical Reports Server (13 of the documents new to the reference list). In the page built from commit `bd0b075`, the answer files for the ISS cabin and the lunar habitat quote 61 of the 66 in these lists.
4. **Where the evidence stops for this need.** The gaps in oxygen, pressure and gravity, the statements in documents that something is not known, and the searches that found nothing.

The sentences that frame these four parts are fixed sentences written by code. The product's language model wrote none of them.

### How it works

**Data pipeline.** Written specifications (JSON files) say which column of each PSI table maps to which standard variable, by which rule and in which unit. Code then reads 14 tables of 12 investigations into one table with one row per test: 1,824 tests and 18,445 values. Each value keeps its NASA source file and row. 9,226 values are read from a named column of the table. The others are facts that the mapping sets once for a whole table (6,384, for example the fuel phase or the gravity level), uniform material names (1,824) and values computed from other values (1,011). A cell that the rules cannot read stays empty with a flag and is never filled in.

**Needs and the counting rule.** Each of the six needs lists the conditions and outcomes a test must record, and says what a count means. Five of the six needs are limited to solid fuels by the team. Droplet and gas-flame tests can count for extinction conditions only. The counting rule of Step 2 then applies. The team's files hold 24 links between a need and an investigation. Each rests on a purpose sentence of the investigators or of a NASA project document, with a verdict. 15 links are marked as supported, and only these count. For example, 246 gas-flame tests were recorded at 30 to 40% surrounding oxygen, and none is counted. 92 of them record how the flame ended, but no document that was read states a fire-safety purpose for the experiment. The other 154 record no outcome that the extinction need asks for. Counted tests: flammability limit 133, ignition 0, flame spread and growth 2, extinction conditions 298, combustion products 122, smoke generation conditions 0. These are 408 different tests from 4 investigations, because a test can count for more than one need.

**Ranking (scoring config 1.5.2).** Code reads your words into conditions: oxygen, pressure, flow, fuel, sample shape, research purpose and, for suppression questions, the diluent gas (a gas added to thin the air, such as carbon dioxide). It compares them with each of the 41 test configurations in the team's catalog. A configuration is a group of tests of one investigation that share a range of conditions. Each condition gets a similarity from 0 to 1. The score is a weighted sum of these, from 0 to 100, cut by up to half when the fuel or the oxygen is far off, and multiplied by an evidence factor (data availability and microgravity duration). No language model produces or changes a score. In more detail (`scoring/METHOD.md`, sections 1.2 to 1.8):

- *Weights by kind of question.* The words of the query choose prevention, detection or suppression. The weights of each kind come from the analytic hierarchy process (AHP), a standard method that turns pairwise judgements into weights. Each condition was given an evidence class from cited textbooks, papers and NASA records (decisive, strong modifier, or weak or not documented), and the 51 pairwise judgements follow from the class difference by a fixed rule (1, 3 or 5 on Saaty's scale). Their consistency ratio is near 0 (0, 0.009 and 0.008); that follows from the fixed rule and is not a measure of how good the judgements are. For suppression, the weights of oxygen, pressure, diluent and fuel are also pooled with how much of the variation in NASA's FLEX extinction measurements each condition explains. The judgements were filled in by an AI session; the human lead decided to apply them.
- *Detection by fire signature.* A detection question is matched to what was measured: gas (carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide) or particulate (smoke). NASA defines fire signatures as both. So "CO detection on ISS" ranks BASS-II first (it measured carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide), and "smoke detector on ISS" ranks SAME, SAME-R and SAME-SIM.
- *Compliance lines.* Each condition of the query is checked against each result, with the basis of the value it checked: "met" only for a per-test table value or the catalog's classification, "documented" when a NASA document states it but no per-test value shows it. These lines change no score.
- *Unreported atmosphere.* When a record gives no oxygen, it is scored as 21%; pressure is filled as 101.3 kPa only for experiments that ran in the ISS cabin, the Microgravity Science Glovebox or a Cygnus cabin. Such a result is marked as resting on an assumption, and the assumed values do not count toward its recorded share. For BASS-II a published paper states the pressure, so it is not an assumption.
- *Near-ties.* Results within 3 points of the best remaining score are ordered by recorded share, then by evidence factor, then by score. Five tables whose rows are test days, not tests (the four ACME day tables and SLICE), count as summary tables, with a lower evidence factor (0.95 instead of 0.97).
- *One place for your oxygen.* If your oxygen is not air and none of the top 3 was tested at it, the third place goes to the best result that was.
- *The review of 15 November and the human lead's decisions (B1 to B5, `scoring/METHOD.md` 1.2, 1.5, 1.7 and 1.8; `scoring/WEIGHTING.md` 4.2 and 6).* An AI review of the scorer named five decision items; the human lead decided them at 15:16, and config 1.5.2 states them; it keeps every weight, score and top 3 of the config before it.
  - B1: a "decisive" condition (evidence class 1) gets a large weight; that is not a veto. Only fuel and oxygen have a gate, below a similarity of 0.2. A stricter oxygen gate (0.37) was considered and not taken: on "lunar habitat cotton fabric" it would rank a gas burner first (ACME-BRE, 62.6), because no solid-fuel test reached 30% oxygen. That absence is shown by the test counts, not by the ranking.
  - B2: a value that a NASA document states but no per-test table shows keeps its full similarity. The compliance line marks it "documented", with its basis.
  - B3: without a material the fuel is not compared. The page warns ("the fuel is not compared") and asks for a material; the ranking itself is unchanged. A rule for this text: the top 3 of "lunar habitat" (fuel droplets and gas burners) is the ranking without a fuel comparison, never the best evidence for a habitat.
  - B4: scores of different questions are never compared, because the weights are rescaled over the conditions each question sets. Questions are compared by their test counts.
  - B5: detection questions cover the smoke of materials overheated without a flame, as in SAME and SAME-R. Pressure stays a minor factor there until a source on that smoke is found.

**The raw-data chain.** For each environment and question, code runs five steps over the team's files (`docs/raw_data_design/design.md`; `pipeline/README.md`, sections "What is in the raw files" to "Materials"). No language model runs in the chain.

1. *Question to quantities*: the conditions and outcomes the need asks for.
2. *Placement* of every recorded test, from the table values only: inside the ranges, outside by a recorded value, oxygen recorded for an oxidizer stream, or not placeable from the tables.
3. *Location* of each needed value of each test: in a table cell, in a raw file that was read, in a raw file where only a column header was seen, as on-screen text of a video, in a document sentence, or not found in the files opened (`data/pipeline/question_chain.csv`).
4. *Files by name*: all 60,866 listed files were classified by their names. 12,934 carry the identifier of one test, 30,634 that of a test day or session, and 17,298 none (`raw_file_groups.csv`, `test_files.csv`, `raw_layers_summary.json`). The file-to-test links with the two-label rule are in `file_links.csv`. PSI's own metadata archives do not tie files to tests: in all 24, the per-test study file is the same placeholder.
5. *Archive members by range request*: the member lists (central directories) of 12,600 of the 12,689 zip archives were read by HTTP range requests, 2.05 GB fetched for 12.8 TB of archives, without downloading an archive or opening a member (`archive_summary.csv`, `archive_summary_totals.json`). They list 12,662,319 member files, most of them still images.

Values were read in full from a few raw files only: the 272 FLEX test sheets (250,375 bytes), the BASS as-run workbook (its cabin oxygen and pressure block, which states no place or time of measurement and places no test), 13 Saffire sensor workbooks and two SAME-R workbooks (`raw_values.csv`, `flex_sheet_vs_table.csv`). None of these values enters a count: the counted tests per need are frozen, and a check compares the frozen files by their checksums. The chain then sorts each question into the three piles of Step 3. The reading order is built by the catalog role from the data role's files with a fixed rule (`scoring/reading_order.py`, `data/catalog/reading_order.json`).

Around the chain, the data role added three reference files. `process_steps.json` lists the steps of spacecraft fire-safety work as six NASA documents state them (evaluate a material at the worst-case use environment; a configuration evaluation when a material fails the standard test; the measures named for that case; detection and response), each as a short quote with its source. The order and labels are the team's reading, and the file says "The team recommends nothing." These steps travel in the evidence packages and answer files (JSON); the page does not show them yet. `material_tests.csv` indexes the tests by material and sample form, and `catalog_comparison.csv` compares the catalog's ranges with the pipeline's tests for all 41 configurations (22 of 164 rows differ, 12 after rounding to the catalog's printed digits; information for the catalog role).

**Evidence packages and answer files.** For each environment, the pipeline writes one evidence package: for each need, the matched tests, their values, the gaps, the values the tests showed and the reference items. There are 6 environment packages and 20 packages for single cells of oxygen and pressure. The need-by-need text of Step 3 comes from answer files that code writes from the same tables and from 334 published statements (212 results, 55 criteria, 55 stated gaps and 12 caveats, from 246 items of the reference list), each tagged by the conditions its own words state. The page offers the ISS-cabin and lunar-habitat packages as JSON, next to a CSV of the matched tests (246 and 30 tests) and the list of dataset DOIs.

**Results first.** On 15 November the data role arranged what NASA's experiments found, as published, question by question (`pipeline/results.py`; `pipeline/README.md`, "Results first"). `data/pipeline/results_by_need.json` holds one block for each of 6 environments and 6 needs (36 blocks): the quoted results for the need, then the criteria as quotations, the steps of NASA's fire-safety process, the recorded tests and the three piles. `results_flat.csv` lists the printed values, one row each (779 rows), and `results_summary.json` holds the counts: 212 published results from 46 documents, 168 of them with printed values (735 printed value rows). Per need: flammability limit 35, ignition 8, flame spread and growth 41, extinction conditions 23, combustion products 18, smoke generation conditions 47, and 45 tagged for all questions. The results are quotations of NASA documents and of the investigators' papers, with the numbers as printed. Where a result is labelled as computed by the authors, as a simulation or as preliminary, its text begins with that label; 29 results of experiments that are not PSI investigations of the catalog (for example Saffire IV to VI) carry a field that says so. Their order is a fixed rule with no weights: first the results whose own words state an oxygen and a pressure inside the environment's ranges, then those that state at least one condition inside and none outside, then those that state none of them, then the others. The team computes nothing from the printed values and compares nothing with a criterion, and a result is not carried from the condition where it was stated to another one. ### Benefits and who it is for

The tool is for engineers who design equipment or a habitat for a crewed spacecraft or base, and for researchers who work with NASA's combustion data: from one description they get the closest NASA experiments, the matching tests to download with their source rows and citations, the raw files to open first for a question, and the conditions where PSI's record holds no counted test (listing of 14 November 2026).

- One description leads to the closest investigations, their data and the way to cite them.
- Tests from 14 different tables can be compared in one table with the same columns and units. Every value traces back to its NASA table row. Values read from a cell also name its column.
- For a question, the page says where the needed values are: in a table, in named raw files, or not in PSI's record. Out of 13.47 TB, a user gets the list of files whose names carry the identifiers of the tests in range (for the lunar habitat, 398 files, 0.77% of the bytes), without anyone processing the archive.
- What the tests showed, what documents state and where the evidence stops stay apart. A reader can tell an observed value from a stated criterion and from a gap.
- Gaps are shown as plainly as results. A reader sees which conditions have no counted test, and which data would close each gap.
- The data leave the page as CSV and JSON, so the analysis can continue in the user's own tools.

### Goals and what is next

The team set four goals. The page serves them as follows.

1. Classify and summarise the experiment data: the catalog of 24 investigations, the result cards with their summaries, the common test table, and the classification of all 60,866 listed files by name.
2. Extract the elements usable for fire safety: the six information needs, the counted tests, the values the tests showed and the files of each test.
3. Give the points to consider for what the user wants to build: the COMPARE step, the requirements read from the question with their compliance lines, and the criteria in NASA documents.
4. Judge whether the data are sufficient and what further data are needed: the three piles per question (in NASA's tables, in the archive, no record in PSI), "Where the evidence stops" and "What PSI does not contain for this environment".

The challenge's own objectives, and what in the product answers each, as far as it goes:

- Investigations most relevant to crewed environments: FIND, the ranking of the 24 investigations for a described environment, and the counted tests for the ISS cabin and the lunar habitat.

### 1. The ranking: rules, not a language model

- What it is: the weighted comparison described under "How it works". Code computes every score.
- Where the rules come from: each rule was compared with one combustion textbook (Turns, 2012) for its direction and its form. Two rules were changed after that comparison, at the human lead's request: oxygen is compared on a ratio scale, and diluent gases by their heat capacity. The textbook does not cover flame spread over solid materials. The rules for sample shape, thickness and flow direction rest on the PSI records and the investigators' papers.
- Where the weights come from: AHP over evidence classes taken from cited textbooks, papers and NASA records (`scoring/literature_evidence.json`, `scoring/WEIGHTING.md`, `scoring/BUILDUP.md`). An AI session assigned the classes; the fixed rule turns them into the 51 judgements. The near-zero consistency ratio follows from that rule and says nothing about the quality of the judgements. The suppression weights are pooled with the NASA FLEX measurements.
- What is the team's choice: the evidence classes, the size of every other value and the ranges. The team kept them after an AI review of each value (`scoring/PARAMETER_REVIEW.md`). Review by combustion researchers is the next step.
- How it was tested: 79 of 79 unit tests; 45 of 45 test cases with an expected answer; first place unchanged in 5 of 5 scenarios when each weight moves by 0.05; 44 of 48 variants keep the same top three in order when each prevention weight is halved or raised by half on four demo queries; the same ranking in Python and in the browser for 106 of 106 queries.
- 8,000 random weightings, sampled again on config 1.5.2: for each of four example queries ("ISS", "lunar habitat", "spacesuit", "Mars base"), the weights were drawn 8,000 times near the team's weights and 8,000 times with no preference. For these queries each result card shows the outcome as its "Weight check" line, behind "Details and sources". For the lunar habitat (no material named), FLEX is first in 88.4% of the draws near the team's weights. For the ISS cabin, BASS-II, BASS and SAFFIRE-III are level in every draw: a tie, not an order.

### 3. The same model in the conversational assistant (prototype)

The contract is `ai/assistant/DESIGN.md`; a reader's version is `docs/assistant/README.md`, and the published methods behind each step are in `docs/assistant/techniques.md`. One turn has six steps, and code sits between the model steps.

1. **Understand** (model and code). The message becomes a structured request in a closed vocabulary (a JSON schema): one of ten intents, the environment, the material, the fire-safety questions and any investigations named. The model reads it twice, with two seeds, and the existing rule parser (`scoring.scorer.interpret`) reads it a third time. A field is taken where the readings agree. A number is kept only if it is in the message, and code converts units. Where the readings conflict, or a needed value is missing, the assistant asks one clarifying question or states the assumption it makes.
2. **Plan** (code). A fixed table picks the lookup tools from the request. The model does not choose them.
3. **Look up** (code). 13 deterministic tools read the team's files and return numbered facts. Each fact has a sentence built from a template, its numbers, its source file and a link. On 15 November a further tool, `published_results`, was added to the contract (`ai/assistant/DESIGN.md` 7.10a), so the lookup tools now include it (14 in `ai/assistant/tools.py` of the working copy at 16:14, not yet committed); the frozen question set does not score it. For an environment with a scenario, it reads `data/pipeline/results_by_need.json` and returns, in the data role's fixed order, at most 4 quoted results per question and 6 per turn, each with its document, place and the team's group, and one team-computed count per question. Its fixed limit says that these are quotations, that the order is the team's fixed rule with no weights, and that the team compares nothing with a criterion and computes nothing from the values.
4. **Write** (model). At most five sentences, built only from the facts given. Every sentence names the facts it rests on; the fact numbers are a closed list in the schema, so the model cannot cite a fact that does not exist.
5. **Check** (code, then model). 14 code gates test every sentence: it must cite 1 to 4 facts of the turn, every number must appear in the cited facts, quotations must be verbatim, names must come from the facts, and it must hold no safety verdict, advice, prediction, misconception or word such as "guidance". The model is asked once to repair a failing sentence. Then a second model pass, with a fresh prompt that sees only the sentence and its cited facts, answers whether the sentence follows from those facts alone. A sentence that still fails is removed. If nothing trustworthy remains, the answer is put together by team code from the facts, with a fixed label.
6. **Deliver.** The answer, its numbered citations, the panels that fit the question (ranking, counts per question, the three piles, a test table to download, criteria, the files of a test, quoted NASA sentences), the reading of the request as editable chips, and two or three follow-up questions. The dashboard below is driven to the same request.

A turn makes at most four model calls, counted by code. A preset button or a repeated request uses no model call. The conversation is carried as the structured request, not as raw chat history. Text from NASA records and from the user is treated as data, never as instructions. Without a model (`./run.sh --no-model`), the same turn runs with the rule parser and the template answer.

How it is measured: `python3 -m ai.assistant.evaluate` runs a fixed question set (`ai/assistant/eval/questions.json`), written and frozen by its checksum before any model run, through five arms: one model call that gets the facts and answers with no checks (`single_call`), the full pipeline, and the full pipeline with the repeated reading, the code gates or the model checker switched off. The set covers each point of the challenge, comparisons, follow-ups, vague requests that need a clarifying question, requests for a safety verdict or a prediction that must be declined, questions the files cannot answer, questions in other languages and questions that hide instructions in quoted text. The results: measured on 15 November 2026 on the 74 questions of the frozen set (`data/insights/assistant_eval.json`; the table is in `README.md`): the full pipeline against one model call read 94% against 84% of the scored fields correctly, planned the expected lookups in 83% against 66% of the questions, showed 0 against 36 numerals outside the cited facts and 0 against 23 sentences with verdict or advice wording, and declined 6 of 7 against 0 of 7 requests for a verdict or a prediction; the arms without the code gates and without the model checker ran on a fixed half of 38 questions, and the arm without the repeated reading was not run for time. Limits of the measurement: the set is small and was written by an AI session of the team, so it is not a benchmark; the code gates are part of the full pipeline, so the full pipeline scores near zero on them by construction, and the telling comparison is with `single_call` and with the gates switched off; the checker is the same model as the writer, so its verdicts are not independent.

In the assistant, a model-written answer is labelled "Written by a local language model (gpt-oss:20b) from the facts shown, and checked by code. A research exploration tool, not safety guidance." A template answer is labelled as put together by team code, and a replayed answer as recorded, with its date.

### 4. Could a prediction model be trained on these tables at all?

- The question: do NASA's tables hold enough tests to train a model that estimates an outcome? For each outcome column, code asked whether a simple model beats a plain guess (the average) on recorded test conditions held out from its training.
- The rule was fixed in code before any number was computed: at least 100 tests, at least 20 groups of conditions, then a five-fold test in which repeat tests of one condition are never split between training and testing.
- Outcomes tried: nine.
- Verdicts: one outcome passed. For the FLEX droplet tests that ended in extinction, a simple model estimates the ratio of the droplet's size at extinction to its size at the start better than the average does on held-out test conditions inside the table's recorded ranges (mean error 0.126 against 0.1875, 157 tests in 65 groups of conditions). The page calls this "indicative", nothing more. Six outcomes have too few tests or groups. One is listed with its counts only. One cannot be modelled: flow events in BASS-II are noted only when they happened, and an empty cell is not a "no".
- Shipped: counts, recorded ranges, errors and verdicts in words. No fitted model, coefficient or model output is shipped, and none is offered for any condition. As the page says: "None is a cabin-material flammability model."

### 5. The video reader: numbers NASA shows on screen, copied into tables

- What it is: NASA's BASS-II videos show five readings as text on the picture: FUEL, AIR, FAN, RAD and a clock. NASA's ReadMe says that fan voltage and duct flow are "on the video display". As far as the PSI files the team holds show, PSI has no numeric log of these readings. The reader copies them into a table, one row per second, for 19 clips (16,019 rows).
- How it reads: each character cell is compared with 33 stored character templates. A value is written only when every character of the field was read. Otherwise the cell stays empty. It is pattern matching, not a language model.
- The check: 186 frames (80 random and 106 hard cases), about 750 values compared with the picture, none wrong [NOT RECOUNTED: stated in `video/README.md`; the per-frame check sheet is not in the repository]. The frames were read by an AI assistant. It was checked on these 19 clips only.

Nothing from it enters a ranking, a count or a scenario text. The page names it in one labelled sentence on the BASS-II card.

### How the data shaped the project

- The challenge text says the data are hard to find, harder to compare and harder yet to interpret. That gave the three steps of the page.
- The tables differ in columns, units and notes. That led to the common test table and to a written column mapping for each table, so every value can be traced to its table row.
- Counted tests fall in range for 4 of 6 needs for an ISS cabin, for 3 of them on oxygen alone, and for 1 of 6 for the lunar range. That contrast became the main thing the page shows: where the evidence stops. At 30% oxygen or more and at or below 70.3 kPa (10.2 psi), there is no counted test. The 17 counted droplet tests lie about 1 to 2 kPa above that pressure.
- The experimental tables are 22 files of 0.2 MB in a listing of 13.47 TB, and PSI's metadata does not say which file belongs to which test. That led to the raw-data chain: start from the question, follow NASA's own identifiers to the files, and say for each question whether the data are in a table, in the archive, or have no record in PSI.
- The records and the investigators' papers state each experiment's purpose. That led to the counting rule: a test counts for a need only when that need is a stated purpose of the experiment.
- The challenge asks for the results of the experiments to be summarised, ranked and interpreted, and no mapped column of PSI's tables holds some of the outcomes the needs ask for (ignition, smoke particle size), while the investigators' papers and NASA's reports print results. That led to the results-first layer: the published results quoted per question, before the counts and the gaps.
- Every test in the common test table was run in microgravity, so the page warns that there are no partial-gravity data. The challenge statement suggests that results at the two ends of the gravity scale could bound what occurs at intermediate gravity levels. The page does not treat them as bounds: in NASA drop-tower tests of three materials (Olson and Ferkul 2012, Table I), the oxygen limits at lunar gravity were lower than at both 0 g and 1 g, and at Martian gravity lower for two of the three; those 5.18 s drops used extinction criteria that differ from the 1 g test.
- For BASS-II, PSI holds videos and no numeric log of the readings shown on screen. That led to the video reader.

## Limits

What the tool does not do:

- It is a research exploration tool. It is not operational safety guidance and gives no safety verdict on a material, an atmosphere or a design.
- It makes no prediction for an untested condition.
- It does not treat results at the two ends of the gravity scale as bounds for lunar or Martian gravity, although the challenge statement suggests they could bound intermediate levels: NASA's own drop-tower results (Olson and Ferkul 2012, Table I, quoted on the page) show oxygen limits at lunar gravity below both. Every recorded test in the tables was run in microgravity.
- It does not judge any test against a criterion and sets no requirements of its own. A compliance line says what a value rests on, not that a design meets a requirement.
- Its rankings, counts, placements, piles and reading orders are the team's computation. They are not conclusions by NASA. NASA does not endorse the tool, and it uses no NASA logo.
- Its test counts cover only PSI's open experimental tables that have one row per test: 14 tables of 12 investigations. 8 more tables are not in the common table. 4 investigations have no experimental table in PSI. The ranking covers all 24 investigations through their records.
- It counts a test for a need only when that need is a stated purpose of the experiment. Other tests are marked "Outside stated objective" and are not counted.
- It gives counts per environment in full for two environments only: the ISS cabin and the lunar habitat.
- A score orders investigations for one question. Scores of different questions are never compared.
- A query that names no material is ranked without a fuel comparison. The page warns about it and asks for a material.

What the published results (results first) are and are not:

- They are quotations of NASA documents and of the investigators' papers, with the numbers as printed. They are not outputs of the pipeline and not counted tests.
- Their order is a fixed rule by the conditions each statement gives, with no weights. A result at an environment's oxygen and pressure may differ from it in material, scale, gravity or flow; the file says which conditions a result does not state.
- The team computes nothing from the printed values (no difference, average or conversion) and compares nothing with a criterion.
- They were collected and quoted by AI agents. Some documents were not read in full text; their entries carry the access label. Reviewed by a person: 0.
- A result is not carried from the condition where it was stated to another one.
- On the page they are shown per question in the file's own order; `results_top.json`, the five to show first, is a data file that the page does not print today.

What the raw-data chain does not claim:

- That the archive was analysed or indexed as a whole: files were classified by their names, and only a few were opened.
- That a file whose name carries a test's identifier holds what a question needs.
- That "not found in the files opened" means absent from the archive.
- That the third pile says what NASA tested: it says what PSI's record held on 14 November 2026, on one of three stated grounds.
- That the reading order is NASA's priority.
- A value read from a raw file is read from NASA's file, not adopted and not counted. A link between a file and a test is "two labels agree", one label, or labels that disagree; disagreements are listed and never resolved.

How far the checks reach (the next step is under "Goals and what is next"):

- The ranking rules were compared with one textbook. It does not cover flame spread over solid materials; those rules rest on the PSI records and the investigators' papers. The evidence classes behind the weights were assigned by an AI session; the size of every other value is the team's choice.
- The six needs, and the links between purpose sentences and needs, were written with an AI assistant from the documents in the reference list. What is the team's own construction is listed in `pipeline/spec/needs.json`.
- All 24 catalog rows were extracted with an AI assistant. The page marks them "catalog values AI-extracted, not yet checked".
- The reference items were transcribed with an AI assistant. Of the 81 documents (82 entries; one paper is listed twice), 70 were read in full, 8 as an abstract or catalogue page only, 1 is known only through a citation, 1 was read by a teammate session and 1 from an unofficial copy. The page names the level where a document was not read in full.
- The expected answers of the ranking test cases were written by an AI session for the team.
- "Written in the source" means the number appears there. It does not mean its meaning was checked.
- The model-written card summaries: reviewed by a second AI session 61, reviewed by a person 0. The code-written sentences on the page: 226 of 226 read by a second AI session, reviewed by a person 0.
- The assistant is a prototype. Its question set was written by an AI session, its checker is the same model as its writer, and its measured results are not final (measured on 15 November 2026 on the 74 questions of the frozen set (`data/insights/assistant_eval.json`; the table is in `README.md`): the full pipeline against one model call read 94% against 84% of the scored fields correctly, planned the expected lookups in 83% against 66% of the questions, showed 0 against 36 numerals outside the cited facts and 0 against 23 sentences with verdict or advice wording, and declined 6 of 7 against 0 of 7 requests for a verdict or a prediction; the arms without the code gates and without the model checker ran on a fixed half of 38 questions, and the arm without the repeated reading was not run for time).
- The independent recount of the raw-data chain reproduced 62 of 71 expected values; the 9 others, all about video link states, are listed and not resolved.
- The video reader was checked on 19 clips only, and its frames were read by an AI assistant.
- The oxygen and pressure ranges of each environment are the team's choice. The lunar range takes pressures below 85.8 kPa, so it extends above the 70.3 kPa (10.2 psi) that the challenge statement names.
- Inside each range is not inside both. For the extinction need, the lunar point (30% oxygen, 70.3 kPa) lies inside the recorded range of oxygen (12 to 34%) and inside the recorded range of pressure (70.2 to 309.3 kPa), each taken alone. Taken together, it lies outside the smallest convex region (the smallest outline without dents) that holds the 273 recorded pairs of oxygen and pressure. All 273 are droplet tests of one investigation, FLEX. The nearest test at 30% oxygen is 0.93 kPa higher in pressure. The region describes where tests were run; it judges nothing. Recomputed by a second AI agent. Reviewed by a person: 0. Files: `data/pipeline/lab/numbers/envelope_lunar_habitat.md` on the branch `data-lab`. (data-lab)
- Pressures that NASA's table gives in mmHg are converted with the torr factor (0.133322368 kPa per mmHg). With the conventional millimetre of mercury, 31 of the 273 FLEX pressures would differ by 0.0001 kPa. No count can change by that difference.
